# Team Geek

Brian W. Fitzpatrick and Ben Collins-Sussman, 2012. I read it after a few
painful episodes where a team seemed to be working hard while still colliding
with one another. I wanted a better account of what good coordination actually
looks like in practice.

## What the Book Argues

The book argues that software teams are not built from personal brilliance alone.
The real work is making it easy for people to be useful to one another. A team
works when it has shared context, honest communication, and clear ownership of
work.

The authors distinguish between the person who is right and the person who is
helpful. Being technically strong is not enough. Teams fail when people hide
uncertainty, avoid asking for help, or wait for a perfect moment to raise the
issue. A healthy team makes friction visible early, while it is still cheap to
fix.

The central idea is that a good engineering culture is created by small habits,
not by slogans. Give people the context they need, keep the work visible, make
your decisions easy to follow, and be willing to say what you know and what you
still do not know. The team becomes easier to trust because the work becomes
easier to reason about.

## Practical Tips

- Make the state of the work visible before it turns expensive. A short update
  naming the problem, the current understanding, and the next question helps the
  team work from the same model of reality.
- Say what you think early, with the trade-off attached. If the issue is likely
  wrong, make that clear and ask for the input that would change your mind.
- Put ownership on the work, not just on the people. A task is easier to pick up
  when someone can tell where the decision actually lives.
- Ask for help when the cost of delay is lower than the cost of guessing. A
  direct request for context is often cheaper than a silent misunderstanding.
- Write down what is done, what is blocked, and what is next. Simple status is
  not bureaucratic theatre; it is a way of keeping the team aligned.

## What It Confirmed

The strongest point in [Communication](../docs/communication.md) is that a
message becomes more useful when it includes what is known, what is unknown, and
what is next. Team Geek makes the same point in social terms. When a teammate
has the problem context, they can help without guessing. When they do not, they
will either overreact or stay quiet.

Good team behaviour is not the same as constant agreement. In [Security Over
Competition](../docs/security-over-competition.md), work gets safer when the
group is allowed to tell the truth without turning it into a contest. That is
what the book means by being a good teammate: speak plainly, do not hide the
cost of a mistake, and make the next step obvious.

A clear owner is also a coordination mechanism. If the question has a place to
land, a task is less likely to become a ghost. That is the same lesson as in
[Deciding](../docs/deciding.md): who decides matters as much as what is being
decided.

## What It Changed

Be direct before the problem becomes expensive. The book makes a good case for
raising uncertainty as soon as it leaves the safe range. I do not need a full
veto to say, "This is likely wrong. Here are the trade-offs and what I need to
check." That keeps feedback cheap and prevents surprise later.

Write down what I know and what I do not know. A short status update is not
just politeness. It reduces the chance that someone will act on a stale model of
reality. That is the visible form of shared context.

Do not confuse familiarity with trust. Familiarity is repetition. Trust is the
ability to rely on one another under uncertainty. The book pushes me toward a
more explicit type of trust: clear ownership, low-status communication, and
committed follow-through.

## What I Rejected

The idea that being a good teammate means being endlessly accommodating. Some
people use helpfulness as a substitute for saying no, and that creates a
different kind of imbalance. A team should be easy to work with, not endless in
its tolerance for bad trade-offs.

The book is also a little too comfortable with the idea that good culture is a
matter of writing the right values down. Values matter, but values without a
working system for feedback and ownership remain decorative. The real change is
in the decisions made when the work is messy.

## Practical Tips

- Share the context before the issue becomes expensive. A short message with the
  problem, the decision, and the risk is more useful than a polished update sent
  after the fact.
- Say what is known, what is unknown, and what is next. This keeps a teammate
  from guessing, and it gives them a narrow place to help.
- Name the owner of the decision when it matters. If the decision is unclear,
  the work becomes a shared ghost and everyone waits for someone else to move.
- Ask for help early when the uncertainty is bigger than the answer. A small
  question is cheaper than a large surprise.
- Leave the next step visible. A task that ends in "I will check" is harder to
  trust than a task that ends in "I will check by Thursday and send the result."

## Review Questions

- Did I explain the problem, my current understanding, and the next question?
- Did I ask for help before the issue became expensive to fix?
- Did I leave ownership clear enough that the work could continue without me?
- Was I honest about what I knew, what I did not know, and what I was
  choosing?
- Did I make the next step easier for the other person to act on?
