# Deep Work

Cal Newport, 2016. I read it because [Meetings](../docs/meetings.md) had already
argued for no-meeting days and think space, and I wanted a better account of why
that time matters than my own irritation.

## What the Book Argues

Newport splits professional activity in two. **Deep work** is done in
distraction-free concentration, pushes your ability to its limit, creates value,
and is hard to replicate. **Shallow work** is logistical, easy to do while
distracted, and easy for anyone to do. His claim is economic rather than moral:
the capacity for depth is becoming rarer at the same time it is becoming more
valuable.

The mechanism is **attention residue**. When you switch away from a task, part
of your attention stays behind, and the next task gets a diminished version of
you. This is why a day made of fragments produces less than its hours suggest.

The book is in two parts: an argument that deep work is valuable, rare and
meaningful, then four rules for getting it.

## The Four Rules

**1. Work deeply. Schedule it, do not hope for it.** Depth does not survive
being left to whatever time is spare, because there is never spare time. Newport
gives four scheduling philosophies that differ only in granularity: eliminate
shallow work entirely, alternate in stretches of a day or more, take the same
block every day, or drop into depth in whatever gaps appear. He notes the last
is his own and is not for beginners, because switching on demand is a trained
skill.

*Tip: book the hard trace as a real block and write the question it should
answer at the top. A block without a question becomes reading, and reading
without an exit condition becomes browsing.*

**2. Embrace boredom. Train the ability, do not just protect it.** Concentration
is not something you have whenever the environment is quiet enough. If every
idle moment gets filled with a screen, the ability to sustain attention weakens,
and no amount of blocked calendar restores it. He suggests productive
meditation: take a walk with one well-defined problem, identify the variables,
and push for a next-step question rather than looping over what you already
know.

*Tip: use the walk between buildings on one open question. Notice when I am
looping over the same known facts, and force the next-step question instead.*

**3. Quit social media. Choose tools by what they cost, not by what they
offer.** He contrasts the any-benefit approach, where any conceivable upside
justifies a tool, with the craftsman approach: name the few things that actually
determine success in your work, and keep a tool only if its help on those
outweighs its harm. Almost anything can be justified under the first test.

*Tip: apply this to channels and feeds rather than to social media. A channel
that has never once changed what I did is costing attention for nothing.*

**4. Drain the shallows. Budget shallow work instead of resenting it.** He puts
the legitimate share at thirty to fifty percent for most knowledge work, and
warns that below thirty you become someone who thinks big thoughts and answers
nothing. To sort the two, he asks how many months it would take to train a smart
recent graduate, with no specialised training in the field, to do the task.

*Tip: use the new-graduate question when asked to take something on. It turns
"which should move?" from a preference into a scale I can say out loud to the
person asking.*

Newport sets his own scope limit before the rules begin. He writes that a senior
executive at a major company probably does not need this advice, and extends the
exemption to some salespeople and lobbyists, calling it a rule of specificity.

## Practical Routine

- Book one sixty- to ninety-minute block before the day fills up. Name it for
  the output, such as "trace why the request retries," rather than "focus time."
- Before the block, write three lines: the question, what enough progress looks
  like, and the first action. If I cannot write the question, spend fifteen
  minutes finding it with someone before protecting time to answer it.
- Close Teams and email, put the phone out of reach, and state when I will be
  available again. Leave one explicit route for something genuinely urgent.
- Keep a piece of paper beside me. When an unrelated task appears, write it down
  instead of switching. Mark each actual switch with a tally and return to the
  written first action or next step.
- If I am stuck for fifteen minutes, write Known, Unknown, Not needed, Next. Ask
  a focused question when the missing fact belongs to someone else; continue
  alone when the next experiment is mine to run.
- Stop five minutes before the block ends. Record the answer, evidence, remaining
  unknown, and exact restart point so the next block does not begin by rebuilding
  context.
- Put shallow work into one or two visible windows. Check messages there instead
  of between steps of the hard work.
- At the end of the week, count completed blocks and inspect what caused the
  first switch in each failed one. Change one condition for the following week.

## Where It Falls Short

**Talking is not shallow work.** A message on Teams that returns a fact I did
not have is noncognitively demanding, logistical and easy to replicate. It is
shallow on every criterion he gives, and frequently the most valuable thing I do
that day. His categories measure the cognitive demand of an activity, not its
leverage.

**The model assumes the problem arrives well-formed.** Every example of depth in
the book is a problem that already exists and needs concentration poured into
it. The hardest work here has started with nobody able to say what needed doing,
and concentration does not produce a well-formed question out of nothing. A
short technical conversation often does. Depth is what I spend after the
question exists.

**Being hard to reach is the wrong correction.** A sender filter assumes the
sender is a stranger with a proposal, not a colleague holding an incident.
[Communication](../docs/communication.md) and
[Work Boundaries](../docs/work-boundaries.md) already have the better version: a
delayed reply with a stated window and a narrow emergency path. That is
availability made predictable, not availability withdrawn.

## Review Questions

- Did I complete at least one uninterrupted hour of deep work today?
- Before I started, did I write the question the block should answer and what
  would count as enough progress to stop?
- How many times did I switch away during the block, and what caused the first
  switch?
- When I stopped, did I record the answer, the next question, or the reason I
  was blocked?
- Did I tell people when I would be available again and leave a narrow path for
  something genuinely urgent?
- Which shallow task created useful leverage today, and which one should I
  decline, defer, or shorten next time?
- Before I protect another morning for this work, how many months would it take
  to train a smart new graduate to do it?
