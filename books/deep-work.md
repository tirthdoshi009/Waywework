# Deep Work

Cal Newport, 2016. I read it because [Meetings](../docs/meetings.md) had already
argued for no-meeting days and think space before I had read any of this, and I
wanted to know whether there was a better case for it than my own irritation.

## What the Book Argues

The book is in two parts, and the second only matters if you accept the first.
Part 1, "The Idea," argues that deep work is valuable, rare and meaningful.
Part 2, "The Rules," gives four: work deeply, embrace boredom, quit social
media, and drain the shallows.

The definitions carry the argument. **Deep work** is professional activity
performed in distraction-free concentration that pushes cognitive capability to
its limit, creating new value and improving skill. **Shallow work** is
noncognitively demanding logistical work, often done while distracted, creating
little new value. The clause most summaries drop is the third one: deep work is
**hard to replicate**. Newport's case is economic rather than moral. The
hypothesis is that the capacity for depth is becoming rarer at the same moment
it is becoming more valuable, so the few who keep it hold an advantage.

**Attention residue is the mechanism.** He leans on Sophie Leroy's 2009 result:
someone who switches away from a task leaves part of their attention on it, and
performs worse on the next one. Newport uses this to explain why a day of
fragments produces less than its hours suggest.

**The intensity formula.** He offers high-quality work produced as time spent
multiplied by intensity of focus, presented as a law of productivity. It is a
rhetorical device; there is no derivation behind it.

**Depth must be scheduled, and there are four ways to do it.** The monastic,
bimodal, rhythmic and journalistic philosophies differ in the granularity at
which depth is separated from shallowness: eliminate the shallow entirely,
alternate in stretches of a day or more, take the same block daily, or drop into
depth in whatever gaps appear. He notes the last is his own approach and is not
for beginners, because switching on demand is itself a trained skill.

**Measure the behaviour, not the outcome.** Borrowing the 4DX framework, he
separates lag measures, which describe the outcome you want and arrive too late
to change what you do, from lead measures, which track the behaviour that drives
the outcome and can be seen daily.

**He concedes collaboration, then contains it.** Rule #1 contains a strategy
called "Don't Work Alone," which allows that the relationship between depth and
collaboration is tricky. His answer is a hub-and-spoke arrangement drawn from
MIT's Building 20 and Bell Labs: shared spaces where encounters happen, private
spokes where the resulting work gets done. He also defends two people at a
whiteboard as a way of reaching further than either would alone. The concession
is real, and it is bounded. Collaboration is admitted as an input to depth, not
as a kind of work in its own right.

**Shallow work is budgeted, not abolished.** He puts the legitimate share at
thirty to fifty percent for most non-entry-level knowledge work, and warns that
below thirty a person becomes someone who thinks big thoughts and answers
nothing. To sort the two he asks how many months it would take to train a smart
recent graduate, with no specialised training in the field, to do the task.

**Become hard to reach.** A sender filter that makes correspondents do more work
before contacting you; replies that describe the whole process rather than the
next message; and the position that a reply is not automatically owed.

He also sets a scope limit that most summaries omit. Discussing executives whose
value is rapid decisions, he writes that a senior executive at a major company
probably does not need the advice that follows, and extends the exemption to
some salespeople and lobbyists. He calls this a rule of specificity.

## What It Confirmed

The strongest confirmation is one the handbook reached independently.
[Pace](../docs/pace.md) argues that the cost of running two changes at once is
not the second task but "the state that must be held open while both remain
unfinished." That is attention residue, arrived at from queues and review
attention rather than from psychology. Newport supplies the name and the
mechanism for a cost this handbook had already located and priced.

[Meetings](../docs/meetings.md) says progress happens when you have the mental
space to think, and not any other way. The book is three hundred pages arguing
that sentence. Having written it first, then read the argument, I trust the
sentence more and my irritation less.

"Focus time is an existing commitment" in
[Work Boundaries](../docs/work-boundaries.md) is already the operative rule, and
it is stronger than Newport's version because it is framed as a shared
obligation rather than a personal tactic.

The deep and shallow cut is the same cut made by "avoid performative work" in
[Working with Restraint](../docs/working-with-restraint.md), and by the section
in Meetings on busyness against productivity. Newport separates what creates
value from what merely occupies; those documents separate what improves the
outcome from what signals activity.

His rule of specificity confirms Meetings on common sense above all, from an
unexpected direction: the author excludes whole categories of worker from his
own advice, on the first pass, before the rules begin.

## What It Changed

**Budget the shallow work at a number instead of resenting it.** Thirty to fifty
percent. The handbook protects focus in several places but never says what share
of a week legitimately belongs to coordination, review and mail. Without a
number, every interruption reads as an incursion. With one, an overloaded week
becomes the planning information that
[Work Boundaries](../docs/work-boundaries.md) asks capacity concerns to be,
rather than a complaint.

**Quantify depth with the new-graduate question.** How many months to train a
smart recent graduate with no specialised training in this area to do this task?
Work Boundaries supplies the sentence "which should move?" but nothing to answer
it with except preference. This gives the answer a scale that survives being
said out loud to the person asking.

**Add one lead measure.**
[Security Over Competition](../docs/security-over-competition.md) measures
growth by better judgment, deeper technical understanding, clearer
communication and reliable delivery. Every one of those is a lag measure. They
are the right goals and they arrive far too late to change what I do this week.
Keep them; add a single countable weekly behaviour that plausibly drives them.

**Reply with the process, not the next message.** Ask what project a message
represents and what the shortest sequence to close it is, then send that
sequence with the current step marked. [Communication](../docs/communication.md)
already requires a message to say what happens if no response arrives; this is
the same instinct applied to the whole exchange rather than one turn of it.

**When I cannot finish, park it in writing.** Leroy's residue is worst for tasks
left unfinished. Pace already says finish one substantial thing before starting
another. The addition is what to do when that is not available: an explicit
written park, naming the next step, is cheaper than carrying the loop.

## What I Rejected

**Become hard to reach.** This is the part that does not survive contact with
the work. A sender filter presumes the sender is a stranger with a proposal, not
a colleague holding a live site incident. Newport exempts executives,
salespeople and lobbyists by his rule of specificity, and never addresses
on-call, operational or interrupt-driven engineering, which is where the
exemption is most needed.
[Communication](../docs/communication.md) and Work Boundaries already solve this
better: a delayed response with a stated window and a narrow emergency path,
which is availability made predictable rather than availability withdrawn.

**The taxonomy cannot score the message that unblocks the day.** I send someone
a message on Teams, they come back with a fact I did not have, and the task
moves. Score that exchange against his definitions: noncognitively demanding,
logistical, conducted while distracted, easy to replicate. It is shallow on
every criterion he gives, and it was the most valuable thing I did that day.

The handbook already knows this and says it plainly.
[Four Stages of a Task](../docs/four-stages-of-a-task.md) holds that reading is
done "when the cheapest remaining way to reduce uncertainty is another person,"
and treats that handoff as the progress rather than as an interruption to it.

Newport's categories measure the cognitive demand of an activity and not its
leverage, so a five-minute exchange that removes a week of wrong work is scored
by its cost rather than by what it prevented. "Don't Work Alone" does not repair
this, because it admits collaboration only where collaboration feeds
concentration. What is missing is any account of the work that is constituted by
other people rather than interrupted by them: review, handoff, teaching, and the
question that reframes the problem. That work is not shallow, it is not deep,
and the book has no third category for it.

**The model assumes the problem arrives well-formed.** Every instance of depth
in the book is a problem that already exists and needs concentration poured into
it: the chapter, the proof, the dissertation. The work that has actually been
hardest here started with nobody able to say what needed doing, and no quantity
of uninterrupted attention produces a well-formed question out of nothing. A
short technical conversation frequently does. Silence in a room is usually
evidence that the question is not yet askable, rather than evidence that the
problem is novel. Concentration is what I spend once the question exists; it is
not what produces the question.

**The scoreboard of hours spent in depth.** I am taking the lead-and-lag
distinction and rejecting his instance of it. Hours of concentration is an input
measure, and Pace already warns against moving quickly because it resembles
working. Counting hours of depth is the same error one level down, with better
manners.

**The formula as a law.** Time multiplied by intensity is a way of stating a
preference, not a finding. Nothing is measured in those units.

**The weight placed on attention residue.** Leroy's result is narrow: unfinished
tasks, under time pressure, in a lab. Newport generalises it into a claim that
any semi-distracted state is devastating to performance. The generalisation is
his, not hers, and the rest of the book rests on it.

**The deliberate practice evidence as presented.** Newport builds the value
argument on Ericsson. The 2014 meta-analysis by Macnamara and colleagues found
deliberate practice explained under one percent of performance variance in
professions, which is the category this book is about. He acknowledges the
challenge in an endnote, characterises the critics as people inclined to poke
holes in anything popularised by Gladwell, and rests on Ericsson's rebuttal
without reporting the figures. Keep the practice, discount the warrant.

**Depth as a personally held advantage.** "Hard to replicate" is the economic
hinge of the whole book, and it points the wrong way here.
[Security Over Competition](../docs/security-over-competition.md) explicitly
rejects becoming the only person capable of important work, and treats teaching
and documentation as part of the job rather than as interruptions to it. The
README's mantra is to react less and give more. Newport funds the first half
generously and does not fund the second at all.

**The diagnosis, which the author has since moved away from.** He began *A World
Without Email* almost immediately after this book, and in *Slow Productivity*
and two New Yorker essays he relocates the problem from distraction and personal
willpower to overload and the absence of autonomy, and from the individual to
the organisation. He does not recant. But "work at a natural pace" is hard to
reconcile with a tallied scoreboard of deep hours, and the later diagnosis is
closer to what I actually see. Read Part 1 for the case; treat Part 2 as one
person's working arrangements, which is roughly how he now presents them
himself.

## Review Questions

- What share of this week was shallow, and is that inside the budget I set?
- Was the most useful thing I did today something this book would score as
  shallow?
- Does this task have a question yet, or am I about to concentrate on a topic?
- How many months would it take to train a smart new graduate to do the thing I
  am about to spend the morning on?
- Which of the measures I care about are lag measures, and what is the one lead
  measure I can see this week?
- Is this task finished, or parked in writing? If neither, what am I carrying?
- Am I protecting attention, or protecting territory?
- Would the person who needs me right now be able to predict when they can
  reach me?
