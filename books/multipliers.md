# Multipliers

Liz Wiseman with Greg McKeown, 2010, subtitled *How the Best Leaders Make
Everyone Smarter*. The book asks a practical question: does a leader's presence
make other people more capable, or does it make them wait for the leader?

## What the Book Argues

Wiseman divides leaders into **Multipliers** and **Diminishers**. Multipliers use
their intelligence to draw out the intelligence of others. Diminishers keep
important thinking and decisions close to themselves, so people contribute less
than they could.

This is not a distinction between kind and unkind leaders. A leader can be
supportive, energetic and highly competent while still diminishing a team. The
effect matters more than the intention. Giving every answer, rescuing work too
quickly or filling every silence can teach capable people to wait.

The book's central lesson is that leadership is not measured only by the quality
of the leader's own contribution. It is also measured by the capability,
judgment and ownership that the leader develops around them.

## The Five Disciplines

**1. Attract talent.** Find what each person does naturally well and put that
ability to work. Do not collect talented people only to confine them to narrow
roles or reserve the most meaningful problems for yourself.

**2. Create space for thinking.** Make it safe to disagree, ask unfinished
questions and admit mistakes. Keep the standards high without making fear the
source of the intensity.

**3. Extend a challenge.** Give people problems that require them to grow.
State the outcome clearly, show confidence that they can reach it and resist
turning the challenge into a set of instructions.

**4. Debate before deciding.** Put the important assumptions and alternatives
in the room. Invite disagreement before the decision, then make the decision
clear enough that everyone can act on it.

**5. Give ownership.** Name the result, the boundaries and who owns the work.
Remain available without taking the work back at the first sign of difficulty.
Accountability is weakened when the leader becomes the default rescuer.

## Accidental Diminishers

The most useful warning in the book is that diminishing behaviour often begins
with a good intention:

- the idea generator creates so many possibilities that nobody else develops
  their own;
- the rescuer protects people from failure and quietly removes their ownership;
- the pace-setter moves so quickly that others stop trying to keep up;
- the optimist understates difficulty and makes legitimate concerns harder to
  raise;
- the always-on leader brings so much energy that there is no space left for
  anyone else; and
- the rapid responder answers before others have had time to think.

The correction is not to become passive. It is to contribute in a way that
leaves other people with more room, responsibility and confidence than before.

## What to Practice

- Ask the question before offering the answer.
- Speak last when the team already has enough context to reason about the
  problem.
- Give one person ownership of the outcome rather than distributing disconnected
  tasks.
- When someone is stuck, ask what they have tried and what they think the next
  move is before stepping in.
- Treat silence as thinking time rather than as a gap that must be filled.
- Before reviewing the work, agree on the result and the constraints so that
  feedback does not become hidden control.
- After a decision, make the owner and the next action explicit.

## Review Questions

- Did my contribution help someone think, or did it replace their thinking?
- Who spoke less because I entered the conversation?
- Am I holding work because only I can do it, or because I have not allowed
  someone else to learn it?
- Did I give ownership of an outcome, or only assign tasks?
- When somebody struggled, did I coach, clarify or immediately rescue?
- Did the team debate the important assumptions before committing?
- Are people becoming more capable and independent through working with me?
