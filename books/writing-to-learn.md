# Writing to Learn

William Zinsser, 1988. I read it after noticing that the documents in this
handbook which took longest to write were the ones I understood best afterwards,
and I wanted to know whether that was a coincidence.

## What the Book Argues

Zinsser's claim is that writing is not a skill owned by English departments and
applied afterwards to subjects learned some other way. Writing is one of the
ways a subject gets learned at all. He argues it across chemistry, geology,
mathematics, art history and medicine, using passages by practitioners rather
than by professional writers, to show that the clear ones are clear because the
author understood the thing, not because the author had a gift for prose.

The book is aimed at teachers, and its programme is that every discipline should
make its students write. Underneath that is a claim about individuals that
survives outside a classroom: you find out what you know by trying to put it in
sentences, and the places where the sentences will not come are the places where
you do not yet know.

**Writing is thinking made inspectable.** Muddled prose is not a presentation
failure sitting on top of clear thought. It is usually the visible form of
muddled thought, and it cannot be fixed by editing the surface. Zinsser's test
is unsentimental: if you cannot write it plainly, you do not have it yet.

**Sequence is the argument.** Writing is linear, and that constraint is the
useful part. Each sentence must follow from the one before it, so the act of
ordering the sentences exposes the joints where one idea does not actually lead
to the next. Speaking hides those joints; writing cannot.

**Rewriting is where the understanding appears.** He is emphatic that the first
draft is not the thinking written down, it is the material the thinking will be
done on. Most of what a writer learns about a subject is learned in the second
pass, when the sentences that seemed fine turn out not to connect.

**Write as a person, to a person.** Institutional voice, passive construction
and inflated vocabulary are, in his account, mostly defensive. They exist to
make the writer unassailable, and their cost is that the reader cannot tell what
is being claimed or who is claiming it. His recommended correction is to write
to one actual reader in your own voice.

**Simplicity, clarity, brevity, humanity.** Carried over from *On Writing Well*.
He treats jargon as the main enemy: language chosen because it signals
membership rather than because it is the most exact word available.

**You do not have to be an expert to write about it.** Writing is the method for
becoming one, not the report filed once you already are. This is the sentence
that made the book worth reading, because it inverts the order I actually work
in.

## What It Confirmed

The section "Write to Clarify the Work" in
[Communication](../docs/communication.md) is Zinsser's whole thesis, already
stated: writing is not only a way to communicate thinking after it is complete.
I had written that as a practice and he supplies the reason it works.

[Writing Chronologically](../docs/writing-chronologically.md) is his sequence
argument applied to systems. "Do not include steps merely to make the account
look complete" and his insistence that the missing joint stay visible are the
same instruction. His version adds why the discipline is hard: a fluent account
is more comfortable to write than an accurate one with a hole in it.

Known / Unknown / Not needed / Next is a device for finding out what I know by
writing it, which is the book's title read literally.

[Saying I Do Not Know](../docs/saying-i-dont-know.md) gets support from an
unexpected direction. Zinsser is not making an argument about honesty; he is
making one about method. The boundary of knowledge is discovered by writing, so
a person who does not write does not reliably know where their own boundary is.
Stating the unknown out loud is downstream of having located it.

Jargon as protection is the same mechanism as
[Security Over Competition](../docs/security-over-competition.md): language used
to defend standing rather than to transmit meaning.

"Drafting without sending is a valid way to think" in
[Working with Restraint](../docs/working-with-restraint.md) is his position
exactly, one line long.

## What It Changed

**Write the second draft, and read what changed.** I have been treating a
written artifact as finished when it is accurate. Zinsser says the first draft
is the material, not the result. The cheap version of this is to write the
Known / Unknown / Not needed / Next, leave it, and rewrite it once. What moved
between the two drafts is the finding, and it is usually an item that migrated
from Known to Unknown. Ten minutes, and no new work in progress.

**Write the explanation before I understand, not after.**
[Four Stages of a Task](../docs/four-stages-of-a-task.md) orders reading, then
discussion, then understanding, then the work, with writing as the artifact that
ends the reading stage. The book's stronger claim is that writing is how the
understanding is produced, so the artifact should be started while the reading
is still bad. The stages still hold; what changes is that the draft opens the
reading stage rather than closing it.

**Name one reader before writing.** Not the role, the person. A message written
to "the team" acquires institutional voice by default, and institutional voice
is the register in which unsupported claims are easiest to smuggle. Writing to a
named colleague makes an unearned sentence obvious while I am still writing it.

**Treat the sentence I cannot write as the finding.** When a step will not go
into prose, the reflex is to reach for a diagram or a bullet list. Sometimes
that is right. More often the sentence will not come because I do not know the
step, and the list is a way of not noticing. Before reformatting, ask which of
the two it is. Prose stripped of the local nouns is a test I can fail, and
failing it is information.

**Read it aloud before sending anything consequential.** A cheap, slightly
embarrassing habit that catches the sentences whose logic I have not checked,
because the ear notices a missing joint faster than the eye does.

## What I Rejected

**The literary ambition.** Zinsser wants nonfiction to be good enough to read
for pleasure, and much of the book is an anthology of writing that clears that
bar. The unit of work here is a message someone has to act on, and a message
optimised for admiration is a cousin of the performative work
[Working with Restraint](../docs/working-with-restraint.md) exists to prevent.
Take the method, leave the aspiration.

**The blanket case against jargon.** His advice is written for a general
audience, and it does not survive contact with technical work unmodified.
[Writing Chronologically](../docs/writing-chronologically.md) requires precise,
checkable terms: naming the component, the operation and the result. A term that
is the most exact word available is not jargon, and replacing it with plain
English loses the checkability that makes the account inspectable at all. The
distinction I will keep is his real one, which is about motive rather than
vocabulary: a term chosen for precision earns its place, a term chosen to signal
membership does not.

**The curriculum.** The book's programme is institutional, aimed at people who
set what students must write. I am not designing anyone's curriculum, and
adopting the teaching apparatus would add a standing commitment for no gain.

**Writing at length as the default.** His examples are essays, and essay length
suits a reader who has chosen to be there. A colleague reading an update has
not. Length here is a cost paid by the reader, so the drafting is private and
the sending is short.

## Review Questions

- Is this the first draft, and am I about to send it?
- What moved between the two drafts, and was it a Known becoming an Unknown?
- Who is the one person I am writing this to?
- Am I reaching for a list because the structure is genuinely branching, or
  because the sentence will not come?
- Is this term the most exact one available, or does it mainly signal that I
  belong here?
- Would this survive being read aloud?
