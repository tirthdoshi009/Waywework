# A Lens from Jainism

Without being an evangelist, I want to say that I am a Jain. Roughly 0.05-0.08%
of the world is Jain, a minority even among minorities. This document is not a
team requirement or a request to share the belief. It exists so the people I
work with can see where a few of the norms in this handbook come from, in my
own words.

## Origins

Jainism is an ancient tradition; its lineage of Tirthankaras (spiritual
teachers) is traditionally held to predate Mahavir Swami, who lived in the
6th century BCE. Mahavir is the 24th and most recent Tirthankara, not the
religion's founder. He is credited with reviving and clarifying the
principles that Jains follow today, including the ones below.

## Practices

I am not a scholar of the religion, but growing up, a few practices from it
turned out to be directly useful at work.

### Samayika: a bounded vow of stillness

Samayika is a vow to sit without getting up for a fixed period, traditionally
one muhurta, 48 minutes. Someone would lead a chant, and then you simply sat
for the 48 minutes. This was my first real exposure to sustained
concentration.

### Pratikramana: accounting for mind, speech, and body

In Pratikramana, we reflect on and ask forgiveness for harm caused to any
living being, whether we caused it knowingly or unknowingly. We say the harm
may have come through **Man, Vachan, and Kaya**, mind, speech, and body. That
three-way accounting is what stayed with me: it treats an unkind thought the
same way it treats an unkind word or action, and it asks you to own all three,
not just the one someone else could see.

### Equanimity under provocation

There is a well-known account in Jain tradition, from Mahavir's period of
asceticism, in which a cowherd drives nails into his ears while he sits in
meditation, causing him immense pain. He does not retaliate or lash out. The
traditional point of the story is not that he performed an act of forgiveness
in that moment. It is that his equanimity, his freedom from reactive anger,
had already been built beforehand, through years of practiced restraint. By
the time the provocation arrived, there was nothing left to react from.

## What This Connects To in Our Handbook

I am not asking anyone to adopt any of this as belief. I am naming it because
a few of our existing norms are, for me, downstream of these practices:

- Samayika is why I take "Limit Work in Progress" and the idea of protecting
  uninterrupted time in
  [Working with Restraint](working-with-restraint.md) seriously.
- Pratikramana's mind/speech/body accounting is why, when I read
  ["Respond to Mistakes with Accountability"](security-over-competition.md),
  I think accountability should include intent, not only visible impact.
- The equanimity story is the clearest illustration I have for
  ["Pause Before Reacting"](working-with-restraint.md): the pause is built by
  practicing restraint ahead of time, not improvised in the heated moment.

## What This Is Not

This is one contributor's lens, not a team policy. Nobody needs to share this
belief, or any belief, to follow the handbook's actual rules, which are stated
in their own documents without reference to any tradition.
