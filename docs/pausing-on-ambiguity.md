# Pausing on Ambiguity

A problem came up in the middle of the work that nobody in the room could state
cleanly. It was not a hard problem yet; it was an unclear one. The first thing
available to me was a reaction, and the reaction would have been an answer
delivered at the speed of the question rather than at the speed of the evidence.

I stepped back instead, and spent the pause on what would actually work rather
than on what would sound like a position. What came out of it was not a
solution. It was a shape: discussion and development can run at the same time,
some of what we need is already known, some of it is not, and the parts that are
not can be flagged rather than resolved on the spot.

That is the whole move. It is small, and it changed the rest of the session.

## Ambiguity Asks for a Reaction

An unclear problem creates pressure to say something immediately, and the
pressure is strongest on whoever is expected to know. The reaction that comes
out is usually a defence of an earlier choice, a reason the problem is not
really a problem, or a fix invented before the problem was described.

None of those are answers. They are responses to the discomfort of not having
one. The signal to watch for is speed: if my reply arrived faster than my
understanding did, it was the discomfort talking.

Pausing is not delay. It is refusing to let the timing of the question decide
the quality of the answer.

## Ask What Will Work, Not What Will Hold

Once the pause is taken, the question it should be spent on is what will
actually work.

That question is different from what will hold up, what will not be my fault,
or what will end this conversation. Those sound like engineering and are not.
They optimise for my position in the room, and they produce designs that survive
the meeting and fail afterwards.

"What will work" has the useful property of being answerable by evidence rather
than by argument, which is also what makes it calming. It moves the problem from
something being done to me to something in front of both of us.

## Discussion and Development Are Not Sequential

The instinct with an ambiguous problem is to stop building until it is settled.
That is usually wrong, and it is expensive, because the discussion often needs
the thing being built in order to become concrete.

Let both run. Continue the part of the work that no version of the answer would
throw away, and keep the ambiguity in open discussion beside it. What stops is
only the work that a plausible answer would waste.

This requires being honest about which is which. Work that all candidate answers
keep is safe to continue; work that exists only under one answer should wait for
the answer. Continuing everything is not parallelism, it is a bet.

## Separate What Is Known From What Is Not

Most ambiguous problems are not uniformly unclear. A real portion of them is
already known, and the unclear portion is smaller than the feeling it creates.

Say both parts out loud. Here is what we know and can act on today. Here is what
we do not know. Naming the known part is not padding; it is what makes the
unknown part small enough to hold, and it stops the room from treating the whole
problem as unsettled.

The written version of this split is the Known / Unknown / Not needed / Next
structure in [Communication](communication.md).

## Flagging an Unknown Is Enough

An unknown does not have to be resolved in the moment it appears. It has to be
made visible, so that nobody plans against it as though it were settled.

"I do not know this yet, and I am flagging it" is a complete contribution. It
costs one sentence, it is accurate, and it converts a private worry into shared
work. The failure mode is the opposite: carrying the unknown silently until it
turns into rework, which is what
[Saying I Do Not Know](saying-i-dont-know.md) covers.

Flag it, attach a question to it, and let it be owned. Do not let its presence
become a reason to stop, and do not let it be absorbed downstream as certainty.

## What the Pause Actually Buys

The pause is worth taking for what it does to me, not only for what it does to
the problem.

It makes me non-reactive: I stopped protecting a position and started looking at
the work. It also means the next thing I say is something I can stand behind
tomorrow, which is the version that other people can build on. The cost was a
few seconds of silence that nobody in the room noticed.

## Review Questions

- Did my reply arrive faster than my understanding of the problem?
- Am I answering what will work, or what will hold up in this conversation?
- Which part of this problem is already known?
- What work survives every plausible answer, and can therefore continue?
- Have I flagged the unknown, or am I carrying it quietly?
- Is there a question attached to the unknown, and does someone own it?
- Am I stopping the work, or stopping only the part a wrong answer would waste?
