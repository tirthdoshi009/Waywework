# Four Stages of a Task

I break a piece of work into four stages: reading, discussion, understanding,
and doing the work. Naming them separately is useful because the stages fail in
different ways, and because the trouble is almost always a stage I skipped or a
stage I never left.

Reading is the stage I skip most often. It is also the stage that, when I do
not skip it, never ends on its own. I have spent months reading around a task,
producing a genuinely good map of the problem, while the one question that
would have unblocked it sat unasked. The reading was real work. It was not
progress, because it was never handed to anyone and never resolved into a
decision.

## Reading

Name the question before opening anything. Reading without an exit condition
becomes browsing, and browsing feels like diligence.

Reading ends in a written artifact, not in a feeling of readiness. I use the
Known / Unknown / Not needed / Next structure from
[Communication](communication.md): what the evidence supports, what is still
open, what the evidence does not require me to do, and the smallest action that
would resolve an important unknown. If I cannot write the Next line, the reading
is not finished, however much I have read.

Separate reading that closes an unknown from reading that opens new scope. Only
the first is progress toward the question I named. The second is not necessarily
wasted, but it belongs in the artifact as a deferred question rather than in what
I am doing now.

Do not treat reading nobody can see as having happened. An unshared artifact is
indistinguishable from not having done the work.

## Discussion

Reading is done when the cheapest remaining way to reduce uncertainty is another
person. That is the handoff, and it is the one I miss.

Ask with a default attached. "I am going to do X unless someone objects by
Thursday" is not a claim that X is correct; it is a statement of what happens if
nobody replies. It lets the work move without an answer, and it gives the people
who know better a cheap way to correct me. The default can still turn out to be
the wrong action. What it avoids is having to sound certain before I am.

Reserve the default-and-proceed pattern for reversible decisions. For
consequential ones, name the reviewers and the decision date instead; silence is
not agreement.

Only a blocking unknown justifies interrupting someone. An interesting one goes
in the artifact.

## Understanding

Understanding is not the sum of what I read. I have understood something when I
can state the problem and the shape of the answer to someone who has read none
of the sources, and when I can predict what will break.

Until then I am holding context, not understanding. Holding a lot of context
without understanding is what being lost actually feels like, and more reading
does not resolve it.

## Doing the Work

Start from the smallest step that produces something someone can correct. Decide
what "good enough" means before starting, not after; stopping is covered in
[Working with Restraint](working-with-restraint.md).

If the work stalls, identify which earlier stage it fell out of. Stalled work is
usually an unasked question, not a missing skill.

## Moving Between Stages

The stages are not a schedule and they do not have to run once each. A task can
return to reading after a discussion changes the question. What matters is that
each transition is deliberate:

- leave reading when another person is the cheapest way to resolve what is left;
- leave discussion when there is a decision or a stated default;
- leave understanding when the work can be explained without the sources; and
- leave the work when the intended outcome is met.

Do not run the four stages as a checklist on every small task. Use them when
work has stopped moving and it is not obvious why.

## Review Questions

- Which stage is this task actually in?
- What question was this reading meant to answer?
- Can I write the Next line yet?
- Is the remaining uncertainty something another person can resolve faster than
  I can?
- Have I stated a default, or am I waiting for permission?
- Could I explain this to someone who has read none of what I read?
